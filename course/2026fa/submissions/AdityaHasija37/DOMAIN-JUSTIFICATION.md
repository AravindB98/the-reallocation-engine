# Domain justification — MSIS product roles on STEM OPT

## Executive summary

This page explains who the recipe is for, what they cannot see without it, and where it
saves time in a job-search week. The short answer: an Information Systems master's
student aiming at product-manager roles cannot easily tell which companies have actually
sponsored product roles, or whether those companies are hiring for one right now. The
recipe answers both from records and sends each company to apply, network or skip. By the
student's own estimate, it turns 7.5–10 hours of manual research a week into about 1–2.

## Who, in exactly what situation

An international student finishing an **MS in Information Systems (STEM-designated) in
December 2026**, starting OPT in January 2027 with the 24-month STEM extension (runway to
about January 2030), with **3.5 years of prior work experience**, targeting **Product
Manager, AI Product Manager and Product Analyst roles that ask for 1–5 years**, anywhere in
the US, preferably in tech. For this person the visa clock is not the tight constraint;
landing a product role is. Product hiring at this level leans heavily on referrals.

## The information asymmetry

From the outside, this student cannot easily see:
1. **Which companies have sponsored product titles**, as opposed to sponsoring in general.
   "Product Manager" has no occupation code of its own, so the usual SOC-based sponsor
   lists hide it.
2. **Whether such a company has a matching product opening today**, on a board that is
   really theirs. Automatic guesses go wrong: Iterable's guessed board was abandoned, and
   Payward hires as Kraken.
3. **What the data does not say.** Approval counts are company-wide and undated, and
   E-Verify status is absent. A confident chatbot answer would hide all of this.

## Engine layers

- **80 Days to Stay** (sponsorship history, funding): which companies, and how strong.
- **Job-Ops** (the Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby providers): is there a live matching
  posting.
- **The existing scorer** combines them, with liveness and visa timeline as gates.
- **Cognitive Pivot / BLS** is shown as context only; it carries zero weight in decisions.

## Where it fits the 3-3-2 day

It takes over the **research half of the 2 apply hours**: finding sponsors of product
roles, finding their real job boards, and filtering postings by title, level and
location. It also feeds the **3 networking hours**: companies that sponsor product roles
but have no opening go to an outreach-first list ("find 2 product people, ask for a
20-minute chat now"). The resulting project, with tests and honest limits, belongs to the
**3 credibility hours**.

**Time saved (an estimate, not a measurement).** By hand, I need about 15 minutes per
company (shorter or longer depending on company size) to check sponsorship, find the
careers page and scan for product roles, and I would research at most 30–40 companies a
week. That is **about 7.5–10 hours a week**. With the recipe, the per-company work drops
to a one-time board confirmation (assumed about 3 minutes; not timed) plus reading the
report: about **1.5–2 hours in the first week, and well under an hour in later weeks**,
since confirmed boards are reused. Scanning job boards company by company is not a good
way to find roles unless you already have target companies. This recipe produces that
target list.

## Failure modes specific to this domain

1. **"Proven sponsor" read as "proven sponsor of PMs."** The approval count is
   company-wide. Datadog's 340 approvals are mostly engineering titles, with one
   "PRODUCT MANAGER" among its top five. *Hardest to catch for:* a student new to H-1B
   data, who sees "Proven · 340 approvals" next to a PM posting and assumes PM sponsorship
   is routine there.
2. **A PM role that doesn't qualify for STEM OPT.** STEM OPT requires the job to be
   directly related to the STEM degree, backed by an employer training plan (Form I-983)
   and an E-Verify employer. A business-heavy PM role may not satisfy that for an MSIS
   degree. The tool checks none of this. *Hardest to catch for:* the student and a busy
   hiring manager alike, because it surfaces only at onboarding, after the offer.
